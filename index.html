MODULE 01 • CLASSICAL & QUANTUM OPTICS

Wave Interference & Young's Experiment

In 1801, polymath Thomas Young performed an experiment that shook the foundations of classical Newtonian mechanics: by passing monochromatic light through two closely spaced parallel slits, he proved conclusively that light behaves as a continuous wave.

🌊 The Principle of Superposition

When a planar wavefront encounters two narrow slits of width $a$ separated by distance $d$, each slit acts as a coherent secondary emitter of cylindrical wavelets (Huygens-Fresnel Principle).

At any given point $y$ on a screen placed at distance $D$, the waves from slit 1 and slit 2 arrive with a path length difference $\Delta x$:

$$\Delta x = d \sin\theta \approx \frac{y \cdot d}{D}$$

✨ Interference Conditions

  • Constructive Interference (Bright Fringes / Maxima):
    $$\Delta x = m \lambda \quad (m = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots)$$
    Peak meets peak; the wave amplitudes add up constructively.
  • Destructive Interference (Dark Fringes / Minima):
    $$\Delta x = \left(m + \frac{1}{2}\right) \lambda$$
    Peak meets trough; waves cancel completely to zero intensity.
📐

Fringe Width Formula ($\beta$)

The linear separation between two consecutive bright fringes (or two dark fringes) on the detector screen is known as the Fringe Width ($\beta$):

$$\beta = \frac{\lambda D}{d}$$
$\lambda$: Wavelength of quantum particle $D$: Distance to detection screen $d$: Slit separation distance
MODULE 02 • THE MEASUREMENT PROBLEM

The Quantum Measurement Paradox & Collapse

What happens when we fire particles one by one? In quantum mechanics, an unobserved electron does not simply pass through Slit 1 OR Slit 2. Instead, its state vector is in a coherent quantum superposition:

$$|\Psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left(|\text{Slit}_1\rangle + |\text{Slit}_2\rangle\right)$$
DETECTOR OFF (Unobserved)

Wave Superposition Pattern

The probability distribution of detecting a particle at position $y$ is given by Born's rule:

$$I(y) \propto |\psi_1 + \psi_2|^2 = |\psi_1|^2 + |\psi_2|^2 + 2\text{Re}(\psi_1^*\psi_2)$$

The crucial term is the interference cross-term $2\text{Re}(\psi_1^*\psi_2)$! This term produces the multi-band alternating interference pattern, even when electrons are fired one at a time. Each single electron interferes with itself!

DETECTOR ON (Which-Way Observer)

Wavefunction Collapse (Decoherence)

If we place a detector at the slits to observe which hole the electron went through:

$$I_{\text{observed}}(y) = |\psi_1|^2 + |\psi_2|^2$$

The phase coherence between paths is destroyed. The interference cross-term vanishes ($2\text{Re}(\psi_1^*\psi_2) \to 0$). The electron acts like a classical bullet, producing two localized clumps directly behind each slit.

MODULE 03 • MATTER WAVES

De Broglie Matter Waves & Electron Diffraction

In 1924, French physicist Louis de Broglie made a daring hypothesis: if light waves can behave like particles (photons, as Einstein showed in the photoelectric effect), then matter particles like electrons, neutrons, and atoms must also possess wave-like characteristics.

⚛️

The De Broglie Wavelength Equation

The wavelength $\lambda$ associated with any moving mass $m$ with momentum $p$ is:

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{m v} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2 m E_k}}$$
$h$: Planck's constant ($6.626 \times 10^{-34} \text{ J}\cdot\text{s}$) $p$: Linear Momentum ($m \cdot v$) $E_k$: Kinetic energy ($e \cdot V$)

🔬 The Davisson-Germer Experiment (1927)

Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer fired slow electrons at a crystalline nickel target and measured the angular distribution of scattered electrons. They observed peak intensity angles identical to X-ray Bragg diffraction, confirming de Broglie's relation with extraordinary precision.

🪐 Why don't we see everyday objects diffract?

A baseball of mass $0.15\text{ kg}$ thrown at $40\text{ m/s}$ has a de Broglie wavelength of:

$$\lambda = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}}{0.15 \times 40} \approx 1.1 \times 10^{-34} \text{ meters}$$

This is $10^{19}$ times smaller than an atomic nucleus! Thus quantum interference is undetectable for macroscopic bodies.

MODULE 04 • QUANTUM FOUNDATIONS

Heisenberg Uncertainty & Bohr Complementarity

Why can't we build a detector so gentle that it detects which slit the electron passed through without destroying the interference fringes? Werner Heisenberg and Niels Bohr showed that nature fundamentally forbids this.

📐

Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle

$$\Delta x \cdot \Delta p_x \ge \frac{\hbar}{2} = \frac{h}{4\pi}$$

🔍 The Mechanism of Disturbance

To determine which slit the particle enters with slit separation $d$, the detector's position uncertainty must be $\Delta x < \frac{d}{2}$.

By the uncertainty principle, this imparts an uncontrollable momentum uncertainty $\Delta p_x \ge \frac{h}{d}$.

This random transverse momentum deflects the particle by an angle $\Delta \theta \approx \frac{\Delta p_x}{p} \ge \frac{\lambda}{d}$, which exactly corresponds to the angular width of one fringe! The fringes are completely smeared out.

☯️ Bohr's Principle of Complementarity

Quantum objects possess mutually exclusive pairs of complementary properties (such as wave-like interference and particle-like trajectory).

The choice of experimental apparatus determines which property manifests:

  • No detector $\to$ Wave behavior observed.
  • Which-way detector $\to$ Particle trajectory observed.
MODULE 05 • ADVANCED QUANTUM MECHANICS

Feynman's Path Integrals & Quantum Erasers

Nobel laureate Richard Feynman described the double-slit experiment as containing "the only mystery of quantum mechanics." In his formulation of quantum mechanics, a particle simultaneously traverses all possible paths in spacetime!

✨

The Path Integral Formula

$$K(b, a) = \sum_{\text{all paths } \Gamma} e^{\frac{i}{\hbar} S[\Gamma]} = \int \mathcal{D}[x(t)] \, \exp\left(\frac{i}{\hbar} \int L(x, \dot{x}, t) \, dt\right)$$

Every trajectory carries an amplitude $e^{i S/\hbar}$ where $S$ is the classical action. When paths interfere constructively, the classical trajectory emerges in the limit $\hbar \to 0$.

🧪 The Quantum Eraser (Scully & Drühl, 1982)

If we mark the photons passing through each slit with orthogonal polarizations (e.g., clockwise vs counter-clockwise circular polarization), which-way information is recorded and fringes vanish.

However, if we place a diagonal polarizer before the screen that erases the which-way information, the interference fringes instantly reappear! This proves that wavefunction collapse is not a mechanical disruption, but rather a consequence of quantum information entanglement.
